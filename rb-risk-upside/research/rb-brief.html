Four Backs, One Error Bar

Swift, Judkins, Montgomery and Pollard go within 22 picks of each other. Their simulated medians span 106 points. Here is what separates them — and how much of that separation is real.

Panel 12,003 RB-weeks, 392 players Simulation 50,000 draws each Backtest 139 player-seasons Updated 19 Aug 2026

Verdict

Both answers are the same player — Quinshon Judkins — and the premise the project was built on did not hold.

The brief hoped to find a back whose workload distribution is more positively skewed than consensus assumes. Three of the four are negatively skewed. The only positive skew belongs to David Montgomery, and it is a long right tail on a 92-point median: a lottery ticket, not a mispricing.

Judkins has the best floor and the best risk-adjusted ceiling. But hold that at 89% confidence, not conviction. The one call this work can genuinely defend is fading Montgomery.

Claims are tagged measured inferred or rejected. Every figure was re-derived from the output data rather than copied from the working notes — thirteen places where a source document disagreed with its own data are listed in the full report.

Resampling the 30 comparables that every distribution rests on gives P(Judkins beats Swift) = 0.89 and P(Swift beats Pollard) = 0.79. Only Montgomery separates with certainty — 1.00 against all three, in every one of 200 resamples.

Montgomery is the actionable one. CBS's projection sits at the 93rd percentile of our distribution — close to our best case, treated as the base case. The mechanism is visible rather than argued: CBS projects 263 carries for a back who took 158 last year, changed teams, and joined a room where Woody Marks handled 196 carries. The comparable evidence says the median outcome for exactly that profile — ageing, reduced role, new team — is RB41, and only 3 of 30 such backs finished top-12. measured

Swift's −43.3 gap is not an indictment of the player. More than half of it — 23.7 points — is simply CBS assuming all 17 games. Most of the rest is 9 projected rushing touchdowns off a 4.04% rate that regresses about 85% of the way back to 3.2%. Discount the projection, not the player. inferred

The availability assumption nobody prices

CBS gives all four backs 17 games. Our simulations give them 13.5 to 14.6. That single assumption silently adds 19 to 27 points to each projection. Corrected to a like-for-like 17-game basis, this work is actually above CBS on Judkins (+32) and Pollard (+16).

What the model cannot do

Stated up front rather than buried, because it governs how much weight any ordering above deserves.

57.4Out-of-sample median error, points — against 58.7 for a curve knowing only ADP
25.5The gap between Judkins's median and Swift's
0 / 19Workload metrics beating a lagged-points baseline after correction

A 25-point median gap sits comfortably inside a 57-point error bar. The point estimate here is 2% better than a naive ADP curve. What survives the calibration test is the shape: an unbiased median (48.9% of outcomes fall below it), a calibrated interquartile range (49.6%), and a bust probability that means something. The distribution is the product; the projection is not. measured

And a clean negative worth as much as any pick: nineteen workload metrics — snap share, carry share, target share, red-zone and goal-line work — were tested walk-forward against simply lagging last season's points. None survives multiple-testing correction. Volume is highly stable (carries per game correlate 0.77 season to season) and still adds nothing, because how a back is used is already inside what he scored. Stability is necessary but not sufficient — the same trap the quarterback research fell into. rejected

Touchdown rate is close to noise: split-half within a single season is r = 0.17, and a 2.3× gap in touchdown rate collapses to 1.1× the following year.

06

What to actually do

  • Fade David Montgomery. The one call this project can defend. The 106-point gap from Judkins is nearly two full error bars; it holds under all six risk metrics, all three role specifications, all five comparable-pool sizes, and in 200 of 200 resamples. P(bust) 0.844, and consensus sits at the 93rd percentile of our distribution.
  • Prefer Judkins at RB22 — as a lean, not a conviction. Best floor and best risk-adjusted ceiling, but held at 89% confidence over Swift, with the raw comparables pointing the other way.
  • Swift is second and the most expensive, going seven picks earlier. You are paying the highest price of the four for the second-best distribution.
  • Pollard only at his price. Worst of the top three on every raw measure, but cheapest by 22 picks.
  • Do not pay for workload metrics. None of nineteen beat lagging last season's points.

If you remember one thing

These four are priced as near-equivalents and are not. But the separation the data supports is Montgomery versus the rest, not a clean 1-2-3-4 ordering. Anything finer than that is inside the noise.
