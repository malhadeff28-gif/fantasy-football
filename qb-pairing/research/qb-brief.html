Fantasy football research · 12-team, 1 QB, standard scoring

The Two-Quarterback Question

Can two quarterbacks with complementary schedules beat one expensive quarterback? Nine phases of testing on eight seasons say no — but they also say something more useful about what a second quarterback is actually for.

Backtest 2018–2025, walk-forward Panel 4,373 QB-weeks Decisions scored 49,026 Data cost $0 Updated 18 Aug 2026

Verdict — stop

The mechanism the thesis proposed does not exist. Schedule complementarity is unmeasurable, opponent difficulty is worth almost nothing, and quarterbacks do not respond to defenses differently in any way that replicates. An earlier draft of this brief argued a second quarterback still paid for itself as insurance. An adversarial re-analysis broke that too — the apparent gain was an artefact of comparing strategies that did not share the same QB1. Held fixed, a second quarterback costs about 11.6 points a season.

Revised 18 August 2026

Sections 8 and 13 previously reported +9.7 and +30.1 points per season for carrying a second quarterback. Those figures were wrong and are retired. The correction, its cause, and two code bugs found alongside it are documented in §14. Earlier numbers are struck through rather than deleted.

Every claim below is tagged measured inferred or rejected. Nothing is asserted that a script in the repository doesn't reproduce.

02

Everything that was tried

Nine studies, each a runnable script. Listed with what it asked and what came back — including the ones that found nothing, which is most of them.

01Can one QB-week be rebuilt from pregame data alone?

Yes. Allen, 2024 Wk 11: projected 19.20, actual 23.98. Leakage audit passes — latest input dated six days before kickoff. The first attempt returned nothing because Buffalo was on bye, which confirmed bye handling falls out of the data model rather than needing a special case.

02Does opponent difficulty predict anything?

Barely. +0.06 pp of start/sit accuracy over recent form; −0.01 pp once the betting market is present. A quality-controlled defensive rating that adjusts for which QBs each defense faced did not rescue it (MAE 6.023 either way).

03Is the draft-day version any better?

Worse. Defensive ratings persist year to year at r = 0.206. A prior-season rating explains 0.39% of QB variance. Easiest vs. hardest third of schedule: 1.00 point per game.

04Which scheduling effects are real?

Wind ≥15 mph (−1.61), home/away (~1.1 spread), indoors/outdoors (~1.1 spread). Not rest, not division games, not time of season. Weeks 14–18 show no effect at all.

05Does game competitiveness shift opportunity?

Not pregame. Pass and rush attempts are flat across spread buckets (32.4/32.6/32.1 and 3.7/3.7/3.7). Actual game script is enormous — losing big costs 3.81 points — but is not forecastable from the line.

06Do QB archetypes respond to defenses differently?

No, at any granularity. League-wide, archetype-level and per-QB matchup adjustments all scored worse than using no adjustment at all.

07What is a second QB actually worth?

+12.0 points a season against a single-QB manager who streams his bye — 25.4% of the perfect-foresight ceiling. Only 57.1% of pairs beat one QB.

08Does it clear the pick and the bench slot?

No. First reported as +9.7 and +30.1, then broken on re-analysis: those compared strategies with different QB1s. Holding QB1 fixed, a second quarterback costs 11.6 points a season, negative at all seven QB1 price points tested.

10Do the surviving findings hold up when attacked?

The economics did not. An adversarial pass reproduced every number exactly, then found the comparison itself was invalid, that no confidence interval had been reported, and two bugs in the pricing code. See §14.

09Is the 2026 pair ranking trustworthy?

No. It reorders completely when its two estimated inputs are varied over a reasonable range. Reported as the headline result rather than a footnote.

03

Which inputs carry signal

Each candidate feature was correlated against the form residual — what a quarterback scored minus his own eight-game average. Measuring against the residual rather than raw points stops a good quarterback on a good team from masquerading as a scheduling effect.

04

The complementarity that isn't there

This is the measurement that ends the thesis rather than merely weakening it. If two quarterbacks' good and bad weeks could be made to offset, their outcomes would be negatively correlated. Across every eligible pair in eight seasons:

+0.008Correlation of two QBs' pregame implied team totals
+0.006Correlation of their actual weekly fantasy points
2,163Pairs measured
2.02 vs 8.09Pregame matchup spread vs. actual outcome spread (SD)

Both correlations are indistinguishable from zero. Quarterback outcomes are already independent. There is no correlation structure for a pairing model to discover, which means the diversification benefit of holding two quarterbacks is the same whichever two you hold. Choosing partners for complementary schedules adds nothing on top of that. rejected

Why this closes the question

A weak predictor can be improved with better features. An absent phenomenon cannot. The pregame matchup signal has a standard deviation of 2.02 fantasy points against outcome noise of 8.09 — it is trying to sort a distribution four times its own size.

The wind numbers were mis-estimated

The original figures were raw bucket means rather than contrasts, and their standard errors treated two quarterbacks in the same game as independent observations — so n = 304 QB-weeks was really 153 games. Corrected, wind ≥ 15 mph is −1.48 (t = −2.53), still real but weaker; wind 8–14 mph collapses to t = −1.29 and is dropped. The claim that weather was the largest scheduling effect also fails: home field is +1.32 (t = 4.38) on the same sample and applies to every game rather than 7% of them.

It is knowable in advance — the betting total is 1.20 points lower in high-wind games, so the forecast was public — but frequency kills it. Wind ≥ 15 mph hits 7.1% of quarterback-weeks, and only 18.5% of within-week pairs are close enough for a 1.5-point tiebreak to change the decision. Upper bound: 0.66 points per season.

Game script: large, real, and useless

The brief hypothesised that close games raise aggression and rushing opportunity. Pregame, they do not: pass and rush attempts are flat across spread buckets (32.4 / 32.6 / 32.1 and 3.7 / 3.7 / 3.7). But how a game actually unfolds matters enormously — losing big is worth −3.81 points (t = −17.8), winning big +2.07.

The clearest lesson in the project

Game script is one of the biggest effects measured anywhere here, and it is worth nothing on draft day or Sunday morning, because it cannot be forecast from the closing line. A large effect and a usable edge are different things.

What the weekly decision is worth

The operational core of the strategy: six start/sit rules, every decision locked before kickoff, run over 1,717 pair-seasons.

Note that "start your better preseason quarterback, every week, without looking" is already right 59.8% of the time. Everything the model knows about markets, venue, form and matchup moves that to 62.9%. measured

The table above compares strategies that do not share a QB1: "late pair" drafts ADP quarterback ranks 10 and 18, while "single value QB" drafts rank 8. Differencing them and calling the gap the value of a second quarterback attributes the QB1 change to QB2. Holding QB1 fixed reverses the sign. Full account in §14.

The insurance argument is also retired. Decomposed weekly, bye coverage is worth +1.0 and the weekly choice +1.7 points per season against a 5.8-point pick cost — and admitting more injured QB1s to the sample lowers the estimate. Injuries explain 8.8% of the original figure. The waiver wire supplies that insurance free.

The surprise nobody asked for

Streaming — drafting no quarterback at all — never had a losing season across the backtest (worst +11.4, SD 20.3) while every drafted strategy did. It also beat drafting a single mid-priced quarterback outright, 36.3 to 12.2. For a manager who dislikes variance it is a genuinely defensible plan.

11

How look-ahead was prevented

Look-ahead bias is the one failure mode that would invalidate every number above, and it is silent when it happens. Four independent guards:

  • Two implementations, cross-checked. One filters history explicitly per week; the other is a vectorised shift-then-roll where every rolling statistic is computed on a lagged series. They agree to 0.0.
  • An explicit leakage audit asserting the latest date feeding any feature precedes kickoff.
  • Walk-forward only. Train on seasons before S, test on S. The projection layer makes this structural, not a convention.
  • Decisions locked before outcomes are seen, in every simulation.

Even the shrinkage target — the league-average baseline that opponent ratings are pulled toward — is an expanding as-of mean, so it leaks nothing either.

13

What to actually do

The tool this research was meant to justify should not be built. Its two jobs would be the weekly start/sit call — worth 4.9 points a season over a rule you can apply mentally — and the pair ranking, which reorders completely under its own assumptions. Neither justifies a frontend.

What the evidence does support, in order of value per unit of effort:

  • Draft one quarterback and do not draft a second. Held at a fixed QB1, the backup costs about 11.6 points a season at every price point tested. This reverses the advice in the first version of this brief.
  • Use the roster spot on a skill position instead. That is precisely the alternative the backup was failing to beat.
  • Do not pay for schedule. One point per game between extremes, unforecastable in August.
  • Cover the bye from the waiver wire. Bye coverage is worth about +1.0 points a season — less than the pick costs, and available free.
  • Consider streaming if you dislike variance. It never had a losing season across the backtest.
  • Do not spend another hour on schedule modelling, matchup interactions, or playoff weighting. They were tested carefully and found empty.

The most valuable output

Nine phases of analysis produced one clearly actionable sentence and a long list of things not to bother with. That second list is worth more than it looks — it is a month of work nobody now has to repeat.

14

What the adversarial pass found

Every KEEP finding was handed to an agent whose only instruction was to break it. The economics did not survive, and the way it failed is more instructive than the original result.

The attack began by reproducing everything: the pick-value curve to 0.0000 from a rebuild that never touched the original code, the strategy table to the decimal, and all three marginals. So the failure is real, not a misreading.

The comparison was invalid

"Late pair" drafts ADP quarterback ranks 10 and 18. "Single value QB" drafts rank 8. Differencing them and attributing the gap to the second quarterback charges it with the effect of changing QB1. Rank-matched — same QB1, with and without a rank-18 partner — the sign reverses at every price point.

No uncertainty had been reported, and it swamps the effect

The +9.7 carried a standard error of 4.8 and a confidence interval of [−3.8, +23.1], p = 0.116. The +30.1 carried a standard error of 34.0 and an interval 189 points wide. Neither published number was ever distinguishable from zero. Both had been quoted to one decimal place.

Two bugs in the pricing code

The pick-cost lookup compared a floating-point ADP against integer bucket edges, so an ADP of 48.1 fell between the 37–48 and 49–60 buckets and was silently charged nothing — 8% of all picks. The identical fix already existed in a later script and had never been backported, which is exactly how it survived. Separately, an unresolved player name deleted a strategy-season rather than charging a bust; the 2021 partner was a quarterback who never played that year.

What did survive

Survivorship bias (2.0% of drafted players fail to join, worth 0.22 points), mean-versus-median pick cost, and 5% trimming. Replacement level was measured rather than assumed: the streaming strategy actually lands at the 56.3rd percentile of weekly starters, not the median.

Why this is in the brief rather than quietly patched

A research document that only shows what survived is not evidence, it is advertising. The original figures are struck through above rather than deleted, so the record shows what was claimed, what broke it, and why. The reversal cost one agent-hour and would have cost a real draft pick.
